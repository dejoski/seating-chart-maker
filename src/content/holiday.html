Do you even need a seating chart for a holiday party?

Not always. For a stand-up open house or a party of a dozen, people find their own spot. A chart starts to help once you have a sit-down meal with more than one table, relatives who don’t get along, or an office party where everyone would otherwise sit with their own team. It takes about ten minutes, and it saves a lot of shuffling at the door.

Family Christmas dinner: tables that work

  • Keep households together. Type couples as “Maria & Joe Rossi” or a guest as “Dan Kim +1” and the tool keeps them at the same table automatically.
  • Seat the hosts where they can get up. Put whoever is cooking at the end of a long table, or the round table nearest the kitchen, and lock them there so auto-arrange doesn’t move them.
  • Spread out the talkers and the quiet ones. Give each side of the family a Group value (“Mom’s side”, “Dad’s side”) to keep branches near each other, or mix them on purpose by leaving Group blank.
  • Grandparents and anyone with mobility needs go closest to the door and the bathroom, not wedged against the wall. Drag them there and lock the seat.
  • One long table or several rounds? A long table feels more like a family dinner but splits conversation into the people beside you. Rounds of 6–8 make it easier for everyone at the table to talk.

The kids’ table

Add a separate table with a name like “Kids” and set its seats. Then decide how strict you want to be:

  • Little kids (under about 6) usually do better next to a parent. Use a keep-together rule between the child and one parent rather than sending them to the kids’ table.
  • School-age kids love their own table. Put a Group value of “Kids” on them so they’re placed together, and seat it within sight of the adults.
  • Teenagers often hate being called kids. A “cousins’ table” with the older teens and the youngest adults goes over better.
  • Siblings who fight get a keep-apart rule, the same as adults.

Office holiday party

  • Mix departments on purpose. Use Group for each team, then decide: leave it on if people want to sit with their team, or clear it and let auto-arrange spread people across tables so they meet someone new.
  • Don’t seat the boss with only direct reports. Put leadership across different tables so each table has a host and nobody feels watched.
  • New hires do better at a table with at least one person they already know. A keep-together rule with their onboarding buddy handles that.
  • Partners and plus-ones stay with their employee automatically if you list them as “Name +1”.
  • Print an A–Z find-your-seat list for the entrance. It’s faster than a table-by-table poster when 60 people arrive at once.

Keeping certain guests apart

Every family has one: the divorced couple, the uncle and the brother-in-law, two coworkers who just had a falling-out. Open the rules tab in the tool above, pick the two names, choose “keep apart from”, and add the rule. Auto-arrange will never put them at the same table, and if you drag one next to the other by hand, both names turn red.

A few tips:

  • Separate tables isn’t always enough. If the room is small, put them at tables on opposite sides of the room and lock those seats.
  • Give each of them an ally. A keep-together rule with someone they like keeps the evening calmer than seating them with strangers.
  • Too many keep-apart rules with too few tables can’t all be met. The tool tells you which rules it couldn’t keep instead of ignoring one quietly. Add a table or loosen a rule.

Quick steps

  1. Paste your guest list into the tool above, one person or couple per line.
  2. Add your tables: rounds, a long table, and a kids’ table if you need one.

  4. Click auto-arrange, then drag anyone you want to move.

Holiday seating questions

Should couples sit next to each other at a holiday dinner?

At the same table, usually yes. Right next to each other is optional, and many hosts split couples across the table so conversation mixes.

How many people per table?

Six to eight at a round table is comfortable for conversation. Ten fits a 60-inch round, but people mostly talk to their neighbours.

Can I reuse last year’s chart?

Your chart is saved in this browser, so if you made it here on the same device it will still be there. You can also edit the names and rerun auto-arrange.

Do I need place cards?

For a family dinner, a printed chart on the fridge is fine. For an office party over about 40 people, table numbers plus an A–Z list at the door work best.
