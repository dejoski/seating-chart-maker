How to do a wedding seating chart without the stress

Most couples leave the seating chart until the last two or three weeks, once RSVPs are in. That’s the right time to finish it, but you can start earlier with the people you already know are coming and fill in the rest later. The tool saves as you go.

  1. Import your RSVP list. Paste it from your spreadsheet or RSVP site export. Columns for Group (for example “Bride’s family”, “College friends”) and Meal are optional but make everything after this easier.
  2. Check couples and plus-ones. “Alex & Sam Lee” becomes two linked guests, and “Jordan Park +1” adds “Guest of Jordan Park”. Linked guests are always seated together.
  3. Seat the fixed tables first. Put yourselves at the sweetheart or head table, seat parents and grandparents, then click the lock on their names so auto-arrange won’t move them.
  4. Add the delicate rules. Divorced parents, an ex, two relatives who don’t speak: add a keep-apart rule for each pair. Add keep-together for friends who don’t share a group.
  5. Auto-arrange and adjust. Guests from the same group are placed together when possible. Drag anyone you want to move; conflicts turn red immediately.
  6. Print and share. The PDF has a floor plan, a by-table list for the planner, and an A–Z list guests can scan at the door. The caterer CSV lists each table with every guest’s meal.

Round or long tables?

Round tables make conversation easy and fit most reception rooms; a 60-inch round usually seats 8 comfortably (10 is tight), and a 72-inch round usually seats 10. Long banquet tables fit more people into a narrow room and feel family-style; a 6-foot table usually seats 6–8 and an 8-foot table 8–10. Always confirm seat counts with your venue, since chair size and centrepieces change what actually fits.

Head table or sweetheart table?

A sweetheart table seats just the two of you and lets the wedding party sit with their own partners. A head table seats you with the wedding party facing the room, which is traditional but can leave partners scattered. Both are available under Tables → Quick layouts.

Wedding seating etiquette, briefly

  • Seat parents and grandparents closest to you, with a clear view of the toasts and first dance.
  • Group guests who know each other, but don’t isolate one lone guest at a table of strangers; give them at least one familiar face.
  • Keep older guests away from the speakers and near the exits and restrooms.
  • A kids’ table works for children over about eight who know each other; younger children usually sit with their parents.

Wedding seating chart questions

Should the seating chart be alphabetical or by table?

For more than about 80 guests, alphabetical is faster to read at the door because people look for their own name. By-table looks nicer for smaller weddings. The PDF includes both, and the sign size guide covers how big to print it.

Do I assign seats or just tables?

Assigning tables is enough for most weddings and is what this tool does. Assign exact seats only for a formal plated dinner, or when the caterer needs to know where each meal goes; the caterer CSV gives them meals by table either way.

When should the chart be final?

Most venues and caterers want final numbers 1–2 weeks before the date. Keep a backup file of each version so you can roll back after last-minute changes.
